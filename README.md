# AisleGuard — Forklift–Pedestrian Conflict Prediction

**Forecasting warehouse collisions ~2 seconds before they happen, from a single fixed camera.**

AisleGuard reframes warehouse safety from *detection* ("there is a forklift and a person") to *forecasting* ("these two are on a collision course"). It projects a standard CCTV feed into real metric floor-space, then uses a custom convolutional network — trained from scratch on bird's-eye velocity rasters — to predict whether a forklift and pedestrian are heading into conflict.

> **Headline result:** on held-out simulated conflicts, the model catches **93% of imminent collisions** versus **0%** for a naive always-safe baseline — while keeping 92.7% overall accuracy.

---

## Why this is a hard (and interesting) problem

Most computer-vision safety projects stop at per-frame detection. But a struck-by alert only has value if it fires *before* impact — which means the system must reason about motion in **real-world distances**, not pixels. Two challenges make that non-trivial:

- **Pixels lie about distance.** A forklift 15 m away and a worker in the foreground can be 40 px apart on screen. Any risk measured in pixels is measuring the wrong thing. AisleGuard solves this with a homography that maps floor pixels to metres.
- **Real near-misses can't be collected.** You cannot safely stage forklift-pedestrian collisions, and they're almost never recorded. So the conflict training data is generated by a physics-based simulator — the same approach autonomous-driving teams use for rare-event data.

## How it works — the pipeline

```
 Camera frame
     |
     v
[1] YOLOv11 detection ---------> person / forklift boxes
     |
     v
[2] BoT-SORT tracking ---------> persistent IDs + motion
     |
     v
[3] Homography ----------------> pixel -> metric floor coordinates
     |
     v
[4] Trajectory corpus ---------> position (m), velocity (m/s) per track
     |
     v
[5] TTC / PET labelling -------> physics-based conflict labels
     |
     v
[6] Procedural simulator ------> 400k labelled conflict scenarios
     |
     v
[7] BEV rasteriser ------------> (6, 64, 64) velocity rasters
     |
     v
[8] BRIN (custom CNN) ---------> SAFE / CAUTION / IMMINENT
     |
     v
[9] Grad-CAM ------------------> explainability overlay
     |
     v
[10] Streamlit demo -----------> interactive + live-video interface
```

### The core idea: physics labels, the network predicts

Time-to-collision (TTC) physics is used to **label** the training data — it is *never called at inference*. At prediction time, BRIN reads the raw bird's-eye raster alone and forecasts risk. This is what lets it generalise to motion a constant-velocity formula can't capture (hesitation, curved approaches), and it's why the network beats a trivial baseline by 93 points rather than merely matching a physics baseline.

---

## BRIN — the model

**B**ird's-eye **R**isk **I**nference **N**etwork: a custom residual CNN, ~491k parameters, trained **from scratch**.

Why from scratch? The input is a **6-channel velocity raster** (person/forklift occupancy + signed x/y velocities in m/s). No pretrained weights exist for this modality — ImageNet features are built for RGB photographs and carry no information about a metres-per-second channel. Transfer learning is conceptually inapplicable, so training from scratch is the *correct* choice, not a shortcut.

| | Accuracy | IMMINENT recall (collisions caught) |
|---|---|---|
| Naive baseline (always SAFE) | 63.1% | **0%** |
| **BRIN** | **92.7%** | **93%** |

Per-class recall: SAFE 0.92 / CAUTION 0.91 / IMMINENT 0.93.

Grad-CAM confirms the network attends to the **collision zone between the two agents**, not a dataset shortcut — and because the input is in metric floor-space, the explanation overlays directly onto the real floor.

## The demo

A two-tab Streamlit app:

**Tab 1 — Risk Model.** Place a pedestrian and forklift, set their velocities, and watch BRIN forecast risk live. Presets ("Collision course", "Safe passing") isolate the model so you can probe its behaviour directly.

**Tab 2 — Full Pipeline (Video).** Upload any warehouse video and watch the complete pipeline run: YOLO detection -> BoT-SORT tracking -> floor projection -> BRIN risk, with the annotated video and a live bird's-eye reconstruction side by side. Track-stitching merges fragmented IDs and predictions are temporally smoothed for a steady readout.

On a random warehouse clip it had never seen, the pipeline detected a forklift approaching a worker and flagged **IMMINENT** — a real near-miss caught end-to-end.

## Key engineering decisions

Full rationale in [`docs/DECISIONS.md`](docs/DECISIONS.md) (16 ADRs). Highlights:

- **Two classes only** (person, forklift) — PPE/pallet classes never enter the risk model, so they were dropped to keep the detector focused.
- **Class remapping by name, not index** — caught a silent bug where one source's `small_load_carrier` was being mislabelled as `person`.
- **BoT-SORT over ByteTrack** — appearance features reduce ID switches; a switched ID fabricates a false velocity and a false alert.
- **Simulator for conflicts** — real near-misses can't be staged; physics-labelled synthetic scenarios solve the rare-positive problem.
- **From-scratch CNN** — no pretrained weights exist for velocity rasters.

## Honest limitations & future work

- **Per-camera calibration.** Accurate metric risk requires calibrating each camera's homography. The video demo uses an approximate frame-geometry scaling for uncalibrated footage, so its floor distances are estimates — clearly labelled as such in the UI.
- **Detection dropout on motion blur.** Fast, dimly-lit forklifts are occasionally missed on individual frames; when either agent isn't detected the system reports SAFE (fails visible-safe, not false-positive). Fine-tuning the detector on more real warehouse footage would address this.
- **ID fragmentation.** Occlusion behind racks can split one track into several IDs. The demo mitigates this with lightweight track-stitching; a production system would add appearance-based re-identification.
- **Simulator-trained risk.** BRIN is trained on simulated conflicts. A real deployment would fine-tune on labelled real-world near-misses.
